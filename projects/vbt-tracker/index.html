VBT Tracker

Velocity-based training from a phone video · no wearable, no encoder

Point a camera at a weight plate, upload the clip, and get bar-path velocity for every repetition of the set. A YOLO segmentation model tracks the plate frame by frame; because every olympic plate is a known 45 cm across, its mask doubles as the pixel-to-metre scale — so the whole thing works with no camera calibration and no hardware beyond the phone already in your pocket.

The model's actual output on one frame: the plate mask, its box, and the confidence. Everything downstream rides on this one measurement.

The plate comes out 470 px across here, and a competition plate is 45 cm whatever weight is stamped on it — so this frame alone fixes the scale at 0.96 mm per pixel. That is the entire calibration step: no checkerboard, no known camera, no measuring anything in the room.

Built for power-oriented training. Bar speed is only a meaningful signal of effort when every rep is moved as fast as intent allows. On hypertrophy work — volume sets taken close to failure at a deliberately controlled speed — velocity loss does not track effort the same way, and the RIR estimate reads misleadingly high.

The Problem

Velocity-based training uses the speed of the barbell, not just the weight on it, to gauge how hard a set actually was. The same load can be crushed at 0.9 m/s on a fresh set and grind out at 0.3 m/s once fatigue arrives — %1RM alone cannot see that, bar speed can.

The standard tool for measuring it is a linear position transducer, and a decent one costs several hundred euros — out of reach for most people training on their own. But every barbell setup already carries a cheap, plentiful, perfectly circular reference object of known diameter: the plates. If a phone camera and a small segmentation model can track one closely enough, it becomes an essentially free substitute for the encoder.

How It Works

01 Detect

A YOLO segmentation model (single class, Disk) finds the plate in every frame, batched 8 frames per forward pass. Ultralytics silently runs a video source at batch = 1 under stream=True unless the batch size is passed explicitly; the detections come back bit-for-bit identical either way, but the pass is ~17% faster on a GPU.

02 Track & scale

The plate's vertical centre and mask diameter are tracked across frames. Since a standard olympic plate is 45 cm regardless of its weight, the measured diameter is the pixel-to-metre conversion — the reason no calibration step is needed anywhere in the pipeline.

03 Segment into phases

A 4-state hysteresis machine turns the normalized height curve into alternating eccentric/concentric phases, then computes average velocity, peak velocity and — for the concentric — the sticking-point velocity: the last point still moving at ≥25% of that rep's own peak, i.e. the speed right before it commits to decelerating into lockout. A fixed edge-trim is not enough here, since a rep that coasts gradually into lockout needs more of its tail excluded than one that stops sharply.

04 Estimate RIR

Each concentric rep's mean velocity maps to a reps-in-reserve estimate through two chained tables from González-Badillo et al. (2017): velocity → effective %1RM, then %1RM → mean reps to failure. An earlier version compared each rep against the fastest rep in the set and was scrapped — it reports a high RIR for whichever rep happens to be fastest even when every rep in the set is objectively slow, measuring relative fatigue rather than actual proximity to failure.

05 Show it

An interactive chart (hover a phase to see its numbers), a per-rep table of duration, velocities and estimated RIR, and a saved PNG snapshot per run — with pipeline progress streaming into the browser live while it processes.

Model & Dataset

The dataset is deliberately tiny

59 images, hand-labeled with SAM in CVAT and exported as YOLO segmentation format, single class. They span different backgrounds, plate colours and brands so the model does not overfit to one look. Segmenting a single high-contrast, perfectly circular object is an easy task — which is also why a small model (yolo26s-seg) rather than a bigger one was the right call.

Architecture

FastAPI + HTMX on the front, a Celery worker doing the actual video processing, and Postgres tracking runs. Progress is pushed to the browser over Server-Sent Events sourced from a Redis pub/sub channel per run — no polling on either the worker or the browser side. 

video upload → FastAPI (POST /runs) → Celery task → YOLO tracking + phase analysis

                                          ├──→ Redis pub/sub → SSE → browser

What It Isn't (Yet)

"Semi-accurate" is the operative word: this has not yet been validated against a real linear encoder on the same reps. Until that comparison exists, the numbers are directionally useful — spotting fatigue, comparing reps within a session — rather than lab-grade. The RIR figure inherits an extra caveat of its own: the thresholds behind it come from bench-press literature and are applied to every exercise regardless, and the velocity fed in includes the deceleration into lockout, so it runs slightly low relative to the mean propulsive velocity the papers were built on.

The next step planned for the model itself is a keypoint model instead of segmentation — predicting the disk's top edge, bottom edge and centre directly rather than post-processing a mask into a centroid and an equivalent-circle diameter. Both should be more stable under motion blur or partial occlusion, where a mask's edges are the first thing to degrade.
